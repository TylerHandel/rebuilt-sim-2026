# REBUILT Sim 2026

A single-player, single-match FRC simulator for the 2026 game **REBUILT**, in the spirit of MoSimulator / CloSimulator.
It runs in the browser (Three.js rendering + Rapier physics), works with an Xbox controller, and simulates **all 504 FUEL**.
Play solo, or against an AI opponent robot (PvE), and build your own autos in the Auto Editor.
The AI can also drive your robot, so you can watch AI-vs-AI matches. Its strategy can be trained by self-play, or by playing Training matches against it, where it also learns from how you drive. You can run defense drills against it, and fine-tune and export its values from the AI Tuning screen.

**Play it in your browser: https://tylerhandel.github.io/rebuilt-sim-2026/** (nothing to download or install; any recent Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari).

## Run it

The easiest way is the link above. Every push to `main` publishes the game there (`.github/workflows/pages.yml`; one-time setup: the repository's Settings → Pages → Source: "GitHub Actions").

To run it from a copy on your computer instead (to work on it, or offline once the libraries are cached), you need Python 3 (already on macOS) and a modern browser (Chrome, Edge or Safari). The 3D and physics libraries load from a CDN, so you need an internet connection the first time.

- **Windows:** install Python 3 from python.org (tick "Add python.exe to PATH"), then double-click `start.bat`.
- **macOS:** double-click `start.command`.
- **Any system:** run `python3 serve.py --open` (Windows: `py serve.py --open`) in this folder and it opens `http://localhost:8765/`.

Keep the server window open while you play; close it to stop.

The menu's last line shows which folder the game is running from. If your copy looks older than the website (robots with their old models, for example), a server from another copy is probably still running: `start.bat` says so when it starts. Close that copy's server window, then start the game again. (On Windows, two servers could otherwise share port 8765 and mix one copy's files with the other's.) Starting a second server for the same folder just opens the one already running.

ES modules won't load from `file://`, so always use `serve.py` instead of opening `index.html` directly.

**Controller:** connect an Xbox controller (USB or Bluetooth) and press any button so the browser detects it. The menus, the match, the Auto Editor and the pause/results screens all work from the controller.

## Cover art

**COVER RENDER** on the home screen (or add `?cover` to the address) opens the cover renderer.
It plays a 3v3 up to TELEOP, lines three robots up in the blue ALLIANCE ZONE with full hoppers, and has them shoot into the HUB. Then it renders that moment on your computer's GPU, a few seconds on a decent graphics card.
The render uses nicer lighting than the game (reflections, arena spotlights), smooth edges, motion blur on the flying FUEL only, and the title.
- **Export:** **Download PNG** (1920×1080, or 3840×2160 under Output), **GitHub preview 1280×640** (for the repository's social preview image), or **Copy** to the clipboard.
- **Shot:** camera angle, height and zoom; motion blur (a shutter from off to 1/20 s; the default 1/125 s is a slight streak); and the moment (how long they've been shooting).
- **Robots:** any of the eleven robots.
- **Title:** the title text, or no title.
- Camera, blur and title changes re-render the same moment. Changing the robots or the moment replays the match.
- Your settings are remembered in the browser; **Reset settings** goes back to the defaults.

**RT is context-aware:**
- With your BUMPERS in your ALLIANCE ZONE, it targets your HUB.
- Anywhere else it lobs FUEL into the nearest corner of your ALLIANCE ZONE, because scoring from outside is a MAJOR FOUL (G407).
- Passes don't wait for a perfect shot: they go as soon as the FUEL would come down on your half of the FIELD, clear of the walls and the HUBS.
- A pass lobs at 55° where that reaches, and flatter (down toward the angle that carries farthest, about 41-44° in the sim's drag model) where it doesn't. A pass from out of reach still goes: flat out at the robot's longest throw, aimed along the line to the pass point, and the FUEL come down short. It waits for the flywheel to be flat out and the hood at that angle, and it may come down short anywhere on the FIELD (no rule says where a pass lands), still clear of the walls and of both HUBS. A robot built of its own mechanisms (2910, 1678, 604, 4414, 1114, 581, 1690, 1706, 8793, 341) sets the speed and hood itself, from its own pass table (past its last row: that row). Where that asks more of its flywheel than its motors give (604's 600 rad/s ceiling), it fires once the flywheel is as fast as it gets (`FlatOut` in `js/mech/physical.js`). 1114's, 1678's and 2910's pass tables are re-found by shooting in the sim (their distances and hood angles, the speed at which the sim's FUEL come down at each row's distance; `THEIR_RPM=1` or `THEIR_PASS=1` runs their own), and rows past what the shooter reaches are flat out and land short. How far each robot's passes go from 10, 13 and 15 m out (`--d` for others): `tools/pass-range.mjs`. 1678 and 2910, landing against the target (m short; their own tables in brackets):

| From the wall | 6 m | 8 m | 10 m | 13 m | 15 m |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1678 (ferry; 13 m: far ferry; 15 m: back to the NEUTRAL ZONE) | 0.0 (1.2) | 0.0 (2.5) | 1.2 (2.9) | 4.7 (5.2) | 1.3 (2.9) |
| 2910 | 0.1 (1.8 long) | 0.4 (2.3 long) | 0.1 (3.2 long) | 0.2 (never fired in 6 s) | 1.2 (0.7) |

1678's passes past about 8 m are out of its reach in the sim (its FUEL slip on the drum: see 1678's shooter below); 2910's past its table's 10 m row ask that row's speed (theirs: flat out).

Every robot shoots on the move: the solver leads the target by the robot's velocity, including air drag.

A shooter fixed to the chassis (2910, 1678, 4930, 581, 1114) aims by turning the whole robot. Its heading loop is linear near the aim (14/s: the swerve follows a turn-rate command within a step or two, so it can be stiff), a time-optimal profile past 16° (slowing at 70% of what the tires turn it at, so no overshoot), plus a feed-forward on how fast the aim moves. The aim leads with the chassis' velocity smoothed over 0.05 s (`AIM_VEL_TAU` in `js/robot.js`). From one step to the next a dumping load and the tires shake the chassis by 1-2 cm/s. Aimed with each step's velocity, the aim flickered by up to 0.75° a step and the feed-forward by 1.4 rad/s either way, turning the robot back and forth as hard as it turns. The load (FUEL resting on the robot, not part of its body) then swung against the hopper's walls and pushed it 1-2 cm/s more, so the heading wandered 3-4° off through a dump standing still and 9-12° weaving. Smoothed, it holds within 0.2° through a dump standing still and 0.1-0.5° RMS weaving at 1.5 m/s, as accurate or better. Turrets aim as before. 971's twin turrets leave the chassis alone while either turret can reach the target; only when the target is outside both turrets' travel (within 30° of straight ahead) does the chassis turn, on the same loop, until one can, firing 0.06-0.08 s sooner than at the old 7/s.

They're matched by file name (start / teleop / endgame or warning / end or buzzer), stay in your browser only, and **Use built-in sounds** switches back.

**Choosing a robot:** the chosen robot's card (its blurb and numbers) sits between two big ‹ › buttons, over a strip of every robot in one row; ◀ ▶ steps along that row, and clicking a team jumps to it. A green dot marks the robots simulated part by part (*Full physics*). 1 v 1 picks the AI's robot the same way, in a smaller strip.

**Options:** an option with a few short values shows them side by side, each one a button (Alliance, On / Off, Sound). In 3 v 3, robots on an ALLIANCE never start on the same spot: each gets the one it asks for if it's free, a *Best for this robot* auto runs mirrored to the other side if only that side is free, and otherwise it takes the nearest free spot. The AIs play as a team: each one watches every robot on the field, drives around teammates as well as opponents, leaves FUEL a teammate is already near, and a defender picks the opposing robot that's the biggest threat (most FUEL, closest to scoring) and sticks with it. The HUD lists every other robot (what it's doing and how much FUEL it holds), and the results show each robot's launched, passed and intaked FUEL.

## PvE: AI opponent

In **1 v 1** an AI robot plays the other ALLIANCE (in **3 v 3**, up to five of them). It can drive any of the eleven robots, scores into its own HUB, and has its own HUMAN PLAYER, who throws when its HUB is active. Its AUTO FUEL counts toward which HUB goes inactive first.

| Strategy | What it does |
|---|---|
| Scorer | Runs its own cycles. It collects FUEL, and **steals** from your ALLIANCE ZONE when it's worth it (every FUEL taken counts twice: one fewer for you, one more for it), then shoots on the move once back in its zone. **Off shifts:** it has one goal, to get as much FUEL onto its side as possible. It collects in the NEUTRAL ZONE and passes everything into its own zone from wherever it is, never over its HUB (from out of its robot's reach the FUEL fall short, still closer to home). It passes in big batches (it collects a load, spins the flywheel up as the load nears the batch size, then dumps it all at once), so a drum robot turns once and any shooter spins up once per batch instead of once per FUEL. Near the end of the shift it fills its hopper and heads home, then works through that stockpile when its HUB turns on. |
| Defense | Tries to **keep you out of your zone**. It guards the BUMP or TRENCH lane you'd use to get in and **rams** you back when you come close. If you get in, it shoves you off your shot. It backs off 72 in before a PIN becomes a foul, and leaves you alone at your TOWER in END GAME. |
| Hybrid | Shift-aware. It defends during the SHIFTS when only your HUB is active and scores the rest of the time. |

How the Scorer collects:
- It goes where it gets the most FUEL per second of driving. A target counts the FUEL around it (up to what still fits), so a big pile a few meters away beats picking through scraps, like the piles that collect in front of each HUB's exits. A trip out of its zone also pays for the drive back. FUEL it can shoot while picking it up (in its zone, HUB active) counts extra, and so does FUEL stolen from your zone. FUEL behind it costs the turn.
- A turret robot collecting in its zone while shooting stays there only while that's still the best place to collect.
- Its intake goes down on the way to the FUEL and stays down while it collects (and on the way home, picking up what's on the way, except 1706: alone, seeds 1-4, it scored 211 TELEOP FUEL on average that way and 227 with its intake up). It comes up only when the hopper is full, when an opponent is about to be inside its reach (allowing for how fast they're closing and how long the intake takes to come up; a latched intake slows the robot instead), or for the moment a FUEL just released by a HUB would drop into it (G408). A robot whose shot compacts its load once its intake is let go (2910, 971, 1678, 4930, 581 and 1114) keeps intaking as the shot starts, as long as its code waits before compacting (1114 0.65 s, 581 0.7 s, 1678 1 s; 0.65 s where it compacts at once) and while FUEL lie in front of it and one has come in within the last second, then lets go so the load is compacted. Alone with the Champs scorer (seeds 1-4, TELEOP FUEL on average, before -> after): 2910 333 -> 353, 971 425 -> 488, 4930 344 -> 354, 581 234 -> 281, 1678 285 -> 281 (two seeds up, two down).
- It fills its hopper all the way before a trip: it's full when the intake stalls against the load. (Rookie and Regional make smaller trips: Cycle fill.)

How the Scorer handles time and traffic:
- It heads in with less than a full hopper only when time runs short. While its HUB is active, it keeps collecting until the last moment it can still get back and empty the hopper before the HUB turns off: every FUEL it picks up until then is one more to score. While its HUB is inactive, it heads in early enough to be staged when the HUB turns on. And at the end of the match it brings in whatever it has.
- Turret robots (4414, 8793, 971, 1690, 4946) don't use a fixed shooting spot. Once inside their zone with the HUB active, they shoot from wherever they are while collecting. Outside the zone they head for the nearest point inside it. 2910 and 1678 still drive to a shooting spot, because the whole chassis has to turn to aim; they turn to face their shot over the last few meters, so they arrive aimed.
- While shooting or passing on the move, it drives smoothly: capped speed, limited acceleration and turning (only while it has FUEL to shoot). This lets the turret, hood and flywheel settle so the shot actually releases (a shot only fires when aim, flywheel speed and hood are all on target).
- If you block it on the way to its zone, it goes around at first. Once it stops gaining ground (you're mirroring it), it drives straight through you.

**Skill** sets its speed, how carefully it collects, how much of its hopper it uses, its shooting accuracy, how long it hesitates between cycles, its reaction time on defense and its pin discipline:
- **Rookie:** slow, small loads, misses more, stops to shoot, and holds pins too long, so it draws G418 fouls.
- **Regional:** a solid district/regional robot.
- **Champs:** full speed, full hoppers, tight cycles and clean defense, using the hand-tuned strategy.
- **Trained (self-play):** Champs-level driving, using the strategy learned by AI-vs-AI self-play (see below).

In AUTO the AI runs a normal routine. Scorer and Hybrid run their robot's *Best for this robot* AUTO. Defense starts beside its HUB, shoots its preload and drives over its BUMP to wait at mid-field, on its side of the CENTER LINE, ready for TELEOP.

### Watch AI vs AI

In 1 v 1, set **Your robot driven by** (under More options) to one of the AI strategies (and **Its AI skill**) to let the AI drive your robot in TELEOP. Your AUTO routine still runs first. In 3 v 3, set every slot to an AI to watch two full alliances. Cameras and pause still work.

## Training mode: teach the AI by playing it

In 1 v 1, set **Match type** (under More options) to *Training (AI learns)*. You play against **Your trained AI**, a Champs-level robot whose strategy ("brain") is saved in your browser. It learns two ways:

- **From results.** Each Training match, the AI plays a slightly different version of its brain: variation A, then its mirror image, variation B, in the next match. After each pair it moves toward whichever did better against you. The results screen explains what it tried and which values changed. **Exploration** in AI Tuning sets how different the variations are.
- **From your driving.** In every match you drive, the game measures the same decisions the brain makes: where you shoot from, how full you get before a cycle, how long you collect, how early you get back for your HUB, how fast you drive through FUEL, how far away you drop the intake, whether you shuttle FUEL in your off shifts and how much you keep, how long you hold a pin, how fast you shove or ram, where you block and when you engage. When you out-drive the AI (win the match, or beat its average in a defense drill), it copies part of your style. **Copy my style** in AI Tuning sets how much.

### Your role: Score or Defense

**Your role** (1 v 1, under More options) can be *Score* (win the match) or *Defense drill*.

In Defense, the opponent always plays Scorer, and your goal is to minimize the points it scores. Fouls you commit count as its points.
- The HUD shows the AI's running score and your target: its average across your earlier drills.
- The results show the points you held it to.
- In Training, the AI learns to score through your defense, rewarded by its own points. When you hold it under its average, it copies your defensive style (pin time, shove speed, block position, engage distance) for when it plays defense itself.

### AI Tuning: see, adjust and export the values

**AI TUNING** in the main menu lists every brain value of Your trained AI. Each value is on a slider (controller or mouse), next to three reference values:
- the hand-tuned (Champs) value;
- the shipped **Trained** value;
- what your driving measured, with its sample count.

It also shows the training record and the learning settings. Buttons:
- Blend toward my driving.
- Reset to the shipped or the hand-tuned brain.
- Clear my driving data, or reset the record.
- Export, copy JSON, and import.

**Exporting into the main version.** When the values are where you want them, press *Export trainedBrain.js*. It downloads a drop-in `js/trainedBrain.js`, plus a `.json` copy. Replace the repo's `js/trainedBrain.js` with the download and commit it. The **Trained** skill level then uses your brain for everyone. You can also:
- keep training it headless: `npm run train -- --from rebuilt-ai-brain.json`;
- import it on another browser.

Pick **Your trained AI** as the opponent skill (or as *Your AI skill* in watch mode) to play or watch it without exploration.

## Self-play training

The AI's strategy lives in a small set of numbers, its "brain". The trainer (`tools/train.mjs`) tunes them by having AI robots play full matches against each other. The brain covers:
- how full to get before a cycle, and how long to collect before scoring anyway;
- when to head back and stage for an active HUB, and where to shoot from;
- how fast to drive through FUEL, and which FUEL to go for;
- how long to hold a pin, how hard to shove and where to block;
- when a Hybrid robot is loaded enough to go defend.

The difficulty handicaps (speed, accuracy, reaction time) are separate, so training improves decisions, not raw ability.

The matches are headless: the same code, field, rules and all 504 FUEL as the browser game, run in Node without rendering. Each match gets a fresh physics world and a seeded random stream, so it plays out identically every time.

How training works:
1. Each generation samples candidate brains around the current one (an evolution strategy with mirrored sampling).
2. Every candidate plays the same seeded scenarios. By default these are 3v3 matches: robots and roles (Scorer, Hybrid, Defense) are drawn at random for both ALLIANCES, all three robots on the candidate's ALLIANCE run the candidate brain, and the other three run a brain from a league (the hand-tuned brain plus earlier snapshots of the trained one). `--teams 1` plays 1v1 matches instead, as it used to.
3. Scores are ranked per scenario, and the brain moves toward the best half.
4. At the end, the trained and hand-tuned brains play the same held-out matches, and the paired difference is reported.
5. The result is saved to `js/trainedBrain.js` (the **Trained** skill level) only if it beats the hand-tuned brain. Each run's history is appended to `tools/training-log.json`. With `--out FILE` it goes to that JSON file instead (`nn-train-cpu.bat` keeps it in `runs\brain.json`, which an update leaves alone).

Requires Node 18+:

```
npm install                                   # three + rapier for Node (the browser still uses the CDN)
npm run train                                 # 10 generations of 3v3 matches, about 3 h on 4 cores (about 45 min on 16 threads)
npm run train -- --gens 30 --pop 12 --scenarios 8 --resume   # longer run, continuing from the last result
npm run train -- --teams 1                    # 1v1 matches (faster: a 1v1 takes about half the CPU of a 3v3)
npm run train -- --quick                      # smoke test, nothing saved
npm run train -- --from rebuilt-ai-brain.json # continue from a brain exported in AI Tuning
npm run match -- --a hybrid:trained:4414 --b defense:champs:2910   # one headless match
```

Each side of `npm run match` is `strategy:skill:robot`. Training options:

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `--gens` | Number of generations |
| `--pop` | Candidates per generation |
| `--scenarios` | Matches per candidate per generation (default 6 in 3v3, 4 in 1v1) |
| `--teams` | `3` (default), `1`, or `1,3` for both |
| `--out FILE` | Save the result to this JSON file instead of `js/trainedBrain.js` (and continue from it with `--resume`) |
| `--validate` | Held-out validation matches |
| `--workers` | Parallel processes (default: all cores) |
| `--seed` | Random seed |
| `--sigma` | Initial step size |
| `--resume` | Continue from the last trained brain |
| `--from FILE` | Start from an exported brain (`.json` or `trainedBrain.js`) |
| `--no-save` | Don't write the result |
| `--force` | Save even if validation didn't show an improvement |

A 3v3 match takes about a minute of one CPU thread (a 1v1 about half that), so more cores train faster.

## Neural-net driver (end-to-end)

The self-play trainer above tunes the scripted AI's strategy values. The neural-net driver takes a different approach: a neural network drives the robot directly, like you with a controller. Ten times a second it chooses the movement, rotation, intake, shoot, pass and outtake, for the whole match.

- **Setup (Windows):** run `nn-setup.bat` once. It installs PyTorch for your NVIDIA GPU.
- **Train fast on the GPU:** run `nn-train-gpu.bat`. It plays thousands of simplified matches at once on your graphics card, each decision replayed as a CUDA graph. Ctrl+C saves; `--resume` continues; `--compile` is faster still.
- **Keep training a network you already have:** double-click `nn-continue-gpu.bat` (GPU) or `nn-continue-champs.bat` (full game against the Champs AIs).
- **Attention and memory:** double-click `nn-train-memory.bat` when progress levels off. It trains a network built differently, in its own run (`runs\memory`): it treats the other robots and the patches of FUEL as separate objects and picks out what matters right now (attention), and it remembers what it saw over the last seconds (which way an opponent is heading, whether a defender keeps following it). It starts by learning from your best trained network, then trains past it in self-play.
- **Train on real matches only (no GPU simulator):** double-click `nn-train-cpu.bat`. First the scripted AI's strategy evolves in generations of 3v3 real matches (an hour or two, first time only). Then a new attention + memory network learns from that AI: the AI rides along with every robot the network drives and says what it would do, the network copies it, then improves on it by trial and error as the AI's pull fades out. It judges each decision by what it does to the whole match (it looks about a minute ahead). It runs as long as you let it; running it again continues. See `tools/nn/README.md`.
- **Train on real matches from a GPU-trained network:** double-click `nn-train-real.bat`. The full game with its real physics, on every CPU thread (the GPU does the learning), starting from your best network trained in the GPU simulator. Much slower, but it learns exactly what the real game rewards. A bigger network to take there: `nn-train-memory-large.bat` first.
- **The strongest recipe:** double-click `nn-train-pro.bat`. It grows a bigger network (1024 × 512) in its own run (`runs\pro`): the new network first learns from your trained one (or from the scripted robot if you don't have one yet), on its own matches, then trains past it in self-play. The trainer's critic sees the whole match and learns normalized returns, and the older versions it keeps playing are the ones that still give it trouble. See `tools/nn/README.md`.
- **Fine-tune in the real game:** run `nn-train.bat --resume --level 3`. It uses every CPU thread for the full simulation and the GPU for learning.
- **Watch progress:** `nn-dashboard.bat` opens the training dashboard: whether it's training right now, its win rates (against the bots, its older versions and the real Champs AIs), how it plays (FUEL, zones, robots), how well it's learning and how fast, with plain-language notes on what's going on.
- **Play with it:** in the menu set **Your robot driven by → Neural net (watch)**, or **Opponent → Neural net**. In a 3v3, set any slot to **Neural net**.
- **Watch it learn:** the dashboard shows a live bird's-eye grid of the matches the GPU trainer is playing. It also shows a real-game scoreboard: while the GPU trains, the CPU plays the latest network in full-game matches against the Champs AIs.
- **New robots and old networks:** what the network sees includes which robot each one is. The 11 robots it first learned keep their places; robots added since (581 on) are recognized in a block appended at the end (observation v4, room for 8 more without changing it again). A network trained before plays exactly as it did, and the trainers grow it to the new inputs (with zero weights) when you resume.
- **Share your network:** double-click `nn-share.bat`. Pick a training run, give it a name and a note, then drag the file it prepares into the GitHub page it opens and click "Commit changes". A couple of minutes later anyone on the website can pick it under **Neural net**: to drive their robot (watch), as their 1v1 opponent, or in any 3v3 slot.
- **Overnight:** double-click `nn-overnight.bat`: GPU training, learning from the pre-programmed AIs recorded in the background, and the real-game scoreboard, all at once.

- FUEL that enters a HUB really falls through it: down the funnel, past the sensors (where it's scored) onto a ramp inside, and out through the exit in the NEUTRAL ZONE face. Inside, FUEL is slick and pressed down a little harder than gravity (`HUB.fuelFriction`, `HUB.extraGravity`), and a roof slopes back from the top of the exit, so the load keeps flowing instead of locking into an arch over the opening: 200 FUEL poured in at 60 a second are all out within about 5 s.
- FUEL that leaves the field is returned by "field staff" near where it left.

**Match:**
- AUTO 0:20, then TELEOP 2:20: TRANSITION SHIFT (10 s), SHIFTS 1–4 (25 s each), END GAME (30 s).
- The alliance that scored more FUEL in AUTO has its HUB inactive in SHIFT 1; status alternates each shift, and a tie is broken randomly.
- FUEL in an inactive HUB scores 0.
- FUEL is still counted for 3 s after a HUB deactivates.
- HUB lights follow Table 5-3: active, 3 s warning pulse, white chase during the TRANSITION SHIFT, off.

**Scoring:**
- 1 point per FUEL in an active HUB.
- TOWER: LEVEL 1 is 15 points in AUTO (assessed at 0:00). In TELEOP, LEVEL 1/2/3 is 10/20/30, assessed at the end, one level per robot.
- MINOR fouls give the opponent 5 points; MAJOR fouls give 15.

**Rules enforced:**
- **G303:** starting position with BUMPERS overlapping the ROBOT STARTING LINE and not touching a BUMP. Preload is limited to 8. Robots start in starting configuration (4414's intake latches down and its hopper extends at the start; 2910's hopper deploys with the intake).
- **G402:** no driver control during AUTO; the robot runs the auto routine you pick.
- **G405** (MINOR, launching FUEL out of the field) isn't called: referees only call it when it's on purpose, and FUEL that leaves the field here always does so by accident.
- **G407** (MAJOR): launching FUEL into your HUB from outside your ALLIANCE ZONE.
- **G408** (MINOR): catching FUEL released by the HUB before it touches the carpet.
- **G425 / G427:** the human player only enters FUEL through the CHUTE or by throwing from the OUTPOST AREA, and only stores FUEL in the CHUTE and CORRAL.
- **R105 / R106 / R107:** robots stay under 30 in, extend at most 12 in, and extend in only one direction.
- Robots can't enter the OUTPOST openings.

**Robot-to-robot rules** apply when an opponent is on the field. Both robots are held to them, and foul points go to the other alliance:
- **G403** (MAJOR): in AUTO, contacting an opponent while your BUMPERS are fully across the CENTER LINE.
- **G415** (MINOR): a deployed over-the-bumper intake reaching inside the opponent's FRAME PERIMETER, i.e. hitting them intake-first with the intake down.
- Ramming, even at full speed, is legal. High-speed contact isn't called in competition, and robots here rock but don't tip over.
- **G418** (MINOR): PINNING an opponent against a FIELD element for more than 3 s, plus another MINOR for every further 3 s. The count resets when the robots are 72 in apart. The HUD shows the pin count for either robot.
- **G420** (MAJOR): in END GAME, contacting an opponent that is touching its TOWER or climbing.

Driving comes from each robot's four swerve modules (`js/drivetrain.js`, `SwerveDrive`), on its rigid body: four Kraken X60s through the modules' gear ratio (2910, 971 and 1678: MK5n R1, 7.03:1; 4414: 7.67:1; 341: 7.67:1 on the 1.8 in wheel radius in their code; the others from their stated free speeds) to 4 in wheels. The robot's mass includes bumpers and battery (58-66 kg). `node --import ./tools/node-env.mjs tools/drivelab.mjs [robot]` measures all of the below.
- **Steering:** each module turns its wheel toward where it's commanded, a Kraken X60 through 150:7 steering gearing, so a 90° turn takes about 0.07 s. A module never turns more than 90°: past that it runs its wheel backwards. While it turns it holds back its wheel's speed, so it doesn't push the wrong way.
- **Driving:** each wheel is driven toward its commanded speed (the teams' velocity loops are stiff), as hard as its motor can push or brake at that speed. The motor's back-EMF and its stator and supply current limits set that, separately: the stator limit caps torque at low speed, the supply limit (on battery current, which is smaller than the stator current until the motor is near full speed) caps it higher up. Each team's limits come from their code where it's public: 2910 100 A stator in AUTO and 120 A from teleop on, 40 A supply; 1678 80 A stator and 40 A supply in AUTO, then 60 A and 30 A from teleop on; 8793, 581 and 604 120 A stator and 40 A supply (604 20 A while launching); 341 a 35 A stator limit and Phoenix's default 70 A supply. In CTRE's swerve code the drive motors' stator limit is the modules' slip current, whatever the motor config says. The rest are assumed 80 A stator and 70 A supply (CTRE's usual settings). The battery sags under all four (and charges back when they brake).
- **Tires:** each wheel pushes on the carpet only within its tire's grip (the friction circle, mu 1.3 x its share of the weight), along the wheel and sideways. Sideways, the tire holds the robot from sliding until it gives. All four are solved together against the robot's mass and spin inertia and put on the body as forces, so the world's step applies them along with whatever the robot is touching.
- **What that gives:** off the line, 90% of top speed in about half a second. A 90° change of direction or a reverse at top speed takes about half a second to be going the new way at half speed: the modules turn, then brake and speed up on the tires. Spinning up to the top turn rate takes about 0.3 s. A robot only goes where its wheels point.
- **Pushing:** two robots shoving meet with their motors' torque and their tires' grip. Equal robots nose to nose stall each other. Driving into one holding still (its drive commanded to stop: the motors brake as hard as they can) creeps it back at 0.1 m/s. The lighter 8793 gets pushed faster, 0.33 m/s, because its tires give first. A robot whose motors out-push its tires spins its wheels.
- **Turning:** driving and turning share the wheels (swerve desaturation), so turning hard at full speed costs speed.
- **Robot cards:** show the real top speed next to the free speed.

Robots ride on their wheels and can pitch and roll: they tilt going up a BUMP, over a DEPOT barrier or onto a jammed pile of FUEL, and the FUEL in the hopper feels the tilt. They only drive with wheels on the floor: a short ray down from each wheel finds the carpet, a BUMP or a DEPOT barrier, and the drive gets that share of its grip, so a robot lifted onto FUEL or another robot coasts instead of climbing. Bumpers follow each frame's shape (4414's cut corners, 4946's round back) and are straight-sided (1.25 in to 6.3 in off the carpet, chamfered underneath and a little along the top), so robots meet bumper to bumper and push level. Everything above is solid too: the frame and superstructure, an extended hopper, a raised lid and the FUEL bulging a net meet other robots and the field (a robot coming off a BUMP into another lands against it, not through it), and intakes meet walls and robots as they come out (one deployed against a wall pushes its robot back). Loose FUEL meet the bumpers, the frame, an extended hopper's box and 581's intake too; FUEL a physical robot's mechanisms have hold of meet its CAD's surfaces instead, so it's only FUEL the intake hasn't taken yet, or beside or under it, that touch these simplified shapes. Where a box's front comes down only so far over the intake's roller in the CAD (1114's crossbar 0.19 m up, 1706's front plate 0.20 m), its collider's front bottom edge is cut back to there (`storage.lip`), as FUEL the roller was lifting into the mouth caught on the box's corner (down the NEUTRAL ZONE's FUEL, five runs each, by 4 s: 1114 80.0 and 77.0 at 1.5 and 3 m/s, from 78.8 and 74.8, with 54 and 39 touching the box at the mouth, from 93 and 79; 1706 62.6 and 61.2, from 61.8 and 60.4, none wedged at 3 m/s, from 16; 1114's AUTOs 106, 109 and 78 AUTO FUEL, from 102, 104 and 77). `tools/fuel-contacts.mjs`, through `tools/intake-test.mjs --contacts` or `tools/team-auto-eval.mjs --contacts`, logs each loose FUEL that touches one of them: where, how fast, and whether the intake took it within 0.5 s. Most of the weight is low (drivetrain, battery), and pitching and rolling are capped and damped the way tires and frame flex soak up a hit, so a crash or a DEPOT barrier at full speed rocks a robot (under 30°) but doesn't flip it.

**FUEL in the robots** (nothing teleports):
- **Intake:** FUEL is only driven by the rollers while it touches them. The rollers grab a FUEL (still a physics ball) only when it touches the spinning front roller (deployed: its front edge at the intake's reach past the BUMPER, its axis level with the middle of a FUEL on the carpet, across its width); they drive it up over the BUMPER, and from the lip nothing powers it: it carries on into the hopper on the speed the rollers gave it, gaining speed as it drops. One that comes off the rollers before it's over (held back by something) is let go, and so is one the rollers stop turning on before it's over: it drops back onto the carpet. The intake's side plates keep FUEL from getting in from the sides, under the intake, where no roller would touch it. (Against the old grab-anything-in-front rule, the AIs pick up about 7% less in a match, mostly FUEL they used to catch at an angle.)
- **Capacity:** a robot holds as much as its modeled hopper physically does, packed the way its intake packs it, up to the team's own number where it's published (then the intake stops taking FUEL there, as a full robot's does). FUEL comes in one at a time at the front, on top of what's there, and the intake roller shoves each one into the load, so the load fills out against every wall, the floor and the nets; the hopper is full when a FUEL stays pressed into the load harder than the roller pushes (`Hopper.pack`, `measureCapacity` in `js/hopper.js`). How hard a roller pushes comes from how much it squeezes a FUEL (`intake.squeeze`: teams report 3/4-1 in with compliant wheels, 1/2-5/8 in with rigid rollers, [Chief Delphi](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/what-is-the-optimal-compression-for-fuel-between-a-2-inch-roller-and-35a-compliant-wheels-2-inch-diameter/511867)), the foam's spring and rubber-on-foam grip: about 60 N (13 lbf) at 3/4 in (`intakePush`). That packing measure (precomputed: `tools/capacity.mjs` writes `js/capacities.js`; a hopper that's changed since is measured live) is only the robot's rated capacity, for the cards, the HUD and the AI's plans. In play nothing counts: the intake stalls. FUEL goes in at the bottom of the pile, on the hopper floor at the front (at the front of a hopper that's out over the intake), not on top of it. A FUEL goes in only once the one before it has moved out of the way; until then the rollers hold it in the mouth and push the FUEL in its way back and up, as hard as they grip it (the same push), so they have to lift the pile over it: its weight (and the FUEL's grip on each other) pushes back. When the load gives way it goes in; the fuller the hopper, the longer that takes, so intaking slows down as it fills. It takes no more FUEL until the load gives (it settles, the robot shoots, a folding intake compacts it). On every preset the robots' aluminum is brushed metal: which parts are aluminum comes from each team's Onshape bill of materials, part by part. `tools/aluminum-colors.py` writes each part's material, and `tools/extract-parts.mjs` puts the aluminum parts of a robot's CAD files in a material of their own, so a plastic part in the plates' color stays plastic (8793's cable carrier, turntable gear and 3D prints share its aluminum's slate). Parts the bill of materials doesn't place, and 971's CAD (made before, from an export that can't be had again), go by color (`js/aluminumColors.js`: the colors in a team's CAD most of whose parts are aluminum). Bare or clear-anodized aluminum is drawn silver whatever gray the CAD uses (8793's and part of 1678's CAD draw it a dark slate), dark-anodized keeps its color, and colored parts (anodized or powder-coated, like 8793's orange tubes) and black-coated ones (8793's rollers) are left as they are. 604's other materials are drawn from its bill of materials too, part by part (its recipe's `bomKinds`): coroplast, birch plywood, 3D prints, rubber, steel and polycarbonate each look like that material (see *604* under *Physical mechanisms*). The field's polycarbonate (the HUB's panels) is clear sheet on High and up like the robots', and its aluminum (the HUB's frames: 107 of the 123 parts in that color in the field's bill of materials are aluminum) is powder-coated: its paint under a glossy coat, reflecting on High and up. The ALLIANCE WALL's glass in front of the Driver Stations (round to the OUTPOST) is clearer than the HUB's on every preset, barely tinted and never hazy, so the field reads through it from the Driver Station camera.

The NVIDIA parts: a browser game can't use DLSS, Reflex or hardware ray tracing (they need a native app and NVIDIA's driver SDKs; WebGL doesn't expose them), so the game runs NVIDIA's real-time techniques in its own shaders instead: **FXAA** (NVIDIA's fast anti-aliasing) on Low, **PCSS** (NVIDIA's Percentage-Closer Soft Shadows: sharp where a robot meets the carpet, softer further off) on Ultra and Max, and **horizon-based ambient occlusion** (the idea behind HBAO+, as GTAO) darkening creases, hoppers and the carpet under bumpers. **Dynamic resolution** (Settings) is DLSS's idea without the AI upscaler: the game drops its render resolution, down to half, while it can't hold 60 fps and brings it back when it can. **Show FPS** puts the frame rate, preset and render resolution in the corner, with where each frame's time goes, in milliseconds and as a share of the frame: physics (the Rapier steps, and how many a second), game logic (robots, AI, FUEL, rules, HUD), rendering on the CPU, and what's left (idle, or waiting on the GPU). Where the browser can time the GPU (Chrome on most desktops), the GPU's own time per frame gets a bar of its own: it works alongside the CPU, so a frame is as slow as the busier of the two (`js/perf.js`). The glow is selective: only lights (HUB lights, robot LEDs) bloom, and whatever stands in front of a light hides its glow. The code is `js/graphics.js`.

## Physical mechanisms (8793, 2910, 1678, 581, 341, 604, 4414, 1114, 1706 and 1690 so far)

The plan: robots built around the physics instead of physics bolted onto robots. A mechanism is shafts turned by real motor models (Kraken X60/X44: voltage, back-EMF, stator and supply current limits) through the gearing in the team's CAD and code, carrying wheels and rollers that move FUEL only by touching it, among fixed surfaces taken from the team's CAD. Every contact goes through the foam's measured spring curve (AndyMark's 2026 testing: their plate test squeezes both sides, so a contact denting one side by d pushes what the plates did at 2d). Past 3.5in of squeeze (the fitted curve's range: 59% of the FUEL's diameter, ~390 lb) it keeps stiffening, as foam does once its cells are crushed shut (densification, the cellular-solids picture: stress growing as 1/(1 − strain/strain_D), strain_D ~0.9): the exponential times (5.32in − 3.5in)/(5.32in − s), so 4 kN at 4in, 11 kN at 4.5in and 45 kN at 5in, on at that slope past 5in. It used to stop stiffening at 3.5in, its force held at ~1.7 kN however far it was squeezed, so a hard enough push put a FUEL through any gap (1114's load went out through the 5.3 cm slot along the bottom of its box's front, squeezed 9.7 cm) and friction where the surfaces slide. The reaction goes back into the shaft (slowing the motor) and the robot. Fixed surfaces push through the foam too, so a FUEL squeezes past a tight spot as a foam ball does instead of jamming like a rigid one. FUEL held in the mechanism touch each other the same way, solved together with the wheels: foam on foam (each dents half the squeeze) with friction (0.6, an estimate), so FUEL pressed together roll and spin each other along instead of sliding. The world doesn't also collide them as rigid balls. No paths, target speeds or forces from nowhere: grip, speed, stalls and jams come out of the contacts. Omni and mecanum wheels use a heuristic for their little rollers (they grip along one direction and roll freely across it). Contacts too quick for one 120 Hz step (a shooter's nip) are sub-stepped: 8 contact steps per step match a 960 Hz run. A FUEL pressed against a wall, a floor or a roller is drawn flattened there (up to four patches, as the hopper's FUEL are), not as a rigid ball pushed through it: its dent is real, the foam's give. The motor controllers' own loops (velocity and position) are closed once per 120 Hz step, except where one step at the motor's current limit changes its speed by more than the error that puts it there (a stiff motor on a light or geared-down load): closed once a step, those overshoot every step and chatter. They close at the Talon's own 1 kHz inside the step instead (`velLoop`, a `Shaft`'s or `Train`'s `vel`): 4414's Dye Rotor, 1678's tunnel and 604's serializer rollers (and all of 1706's velocity loops, its code not being public). Every other loop on the physical robots (the flywheels, drums and launchers, the intake arms' position loops) was checked on the benches and doesn't. Code: `js/mech/mechanism.js`.

### In matches

8793 plays with these mechanisms (`physical: '8793'` in its config; `js/mech/phys8793.js`, on `js/mech/physical.js`, what every physical robot shares). The FUEL it picks up stay bodies on the FIELD the whole way. They're moved only by its rollers, wheels and CAD surfaces, which are mounted on its body, so every push on them pushes back on the robot. Then the shooter flings them, and a shot is counted as it leaves the wheels. Its controls follow their code (ShooterMode):

- The rollers and vertical rollers always run inward, with their automatic jam recovery (`runIntakeWithAutoUnjam`). When the roller motor stalls (over 55 A, under 10 rotations/s) for 0.125 s, the whole path reverses at full for 0.125 s, the turret indexer too. The detector then needs a fresh 0.125 s of stall to fire again.
- The turret indexer feeds whenever there's a shot or pass to make. Like their code (`HOLD_FEED_UNTIL_AT_SPEED` off), it doesn't wait for the flywheel. It only stops while the turret is swinging round to its target, more than 10 degrees off (`TURRET_RESET_TOLERANCE_DEG`).
- Out of its zone it shuttles everything it picks up, all the time (ShooterMode's shuttle). The exception is under a TRENCH, where it switches to its storage outputs. In its zone it scores as the AI decides, like the other robots. A pass goes whether or not it'll get there: flat out at its flattest when the target's out of reach. The only check is that it won't come down into a HUB (G407).
- The flywheel runs on their velocity loop.
- The turret goes endpoint to endpoint over their soft limits, -200 to 270 degrees, with no continuous rotation. Past a limit it swings back the long way round, and the feeder waits. It starts straight ahead.
- **The turret on its motor** (`js/mech/turretDrive.js`, run by `turretStep` in `js/mech/phys8793.js`; the same for 4414). It isn't turned at a set rate any more. It is driven by its X60 (FOC, 40 A stator, 30 A supply) through their 20:200 under their PositionVoltage loop (kP 4, kD 0.25, kV 0.25, kS 0.65 V, the command's own rate fed forward up to 360 deg/s), 8 control steps a physics step. It turns against its inertia (J 0.06 kg·m², estimate), its V-rollers' and gears' friction (4.0 N·m, from their kS) and its energy chain. Bench (`node --import ./tools/node-env.mjs tools/turret-bench.mjs`): its whole travel in 0.83 s to within 2° (0.93 s to stay within 0.6°), 0.86 / 0.96 s with the chain. 10/30/90/180° steps settle in 0.24/0.35/0.51/0.65 s, 0.24/0.36/0.53/0.67 s with the chain, with no overshoot and 0.02° off at rest.
- **The energy chain** (`js/echain.js`, drawn by `js/echainModel.js` in the CAD's track, `cad/robots/8793-track.glb`). It is 46 links of a WCP 15x30 cable carrier (0.92 m: 20 mm pitch, 45° to a joint, 26 mm minimum bend radius). It starts at a ring mount screwed through the outer ring on the right, at the track's stop, and runs counterclockwise round the outside. It then turns back in a U and snakes back round the inside to the base-plate mount under the shooter plate's back-right corner, which turns with the turret. Each frame its pins are stepped along that path at exactly the pitch, and the U's place is solved (secant) so the last pin lands on the mount: the length stays constant and no link passes through another, the walls or the mounts (36.7 mm apart at the closest). One thing doesn't fit the CAD exactly: the track's channel is 50 mm across, and the chain's 39.5 mm links need 46.8° at a joint to turn back across it (a semicircle about the channel's middle), past the 15x30's 45° stop. Bending the U within 45° only works at some phases of the links against each other, and it jumps between them, so the U is drawn as a semicircle 1.8° past the stop. Either their chain isn't a 15x30, or it rides the walls a little loose. Its load on the turret (estimates): the wires' and links' hysteresis 0.10 N·m, the pins 0.016, the U pressing on the walls 0.10, and the inside run sliding on the floor 0-0.09 (how much of it is inside), so 0.22-0.30 N·m of friction in all, about 6% of the turret's own.

What the aim asks for is turned into a flywheel speed with the exit speeds its shots were measured leaving at in AI matches (`SHOT_TABLE`). That's the role their FlywheelDroopCompensator plays on the robot. Fed by the indexer, a FUEL comes into the shooter faster than on the bench and leaves faster, up to 9.2 m/s at 80 rotations/s.

In AI matches:

- **Accuracy:** 75-86% of its HUB shots go in.
- **Volume:** it fires about 320-350 FUEL a match, a lot of them shuttled into its own zone (160-200 points against 4414 and 2910).
- **Intake:** the arm takes in 84-91% of the FUEL it touches; the rest are ones its edge clips.

PathPlanner's trajectory (`pp_path` in `tools/team-autos.py`, 8793's and 1114's) is PathPlannerLib's own, 2026.1.2 (both teams' vendordep), ported from its Java: the path's points about every 0.2 m along its Bezier curves (by waypoint-relative position, then corrected toward the spacing), a point inserted at each rotation target and more where the curve is tighter than 0.5 m; each point's holonomic rotation from the robot's starting rotation through the rotation targets to the goal end state's, cosine interpolated by the distance along the path between one and the next (fastest midway); then the speed per swerve module (each module's own path over the ground, which turning adds to): a forward pass speeding each up as its motor (current-limited, with its torque loss at speed) and its grip (wheelCOF) let it, no faster than its grip rounds its own curve, the four kept in step, the chassis speed and turn rate desaturated to the point's max velocity and max angular velocity (so where the rotation turns fast the robot slows to let it), and a reverse pass slowing it to the goal end velocity; the time between points from their distance and speeds. Rotation isn't profiled on its own: max angular acceleration only caps the angular part of each module's acceleration. FollowPathCommand runs the path's ideal trajectory (from its ideal starting state) if the robot's within 0.25 m/s and 30° of that as the path starts, else one generated from its speed and rotation then (8793's Center Shuttle Path and Shooting Corner). Checked against PathPlannerLib's Python port (made to follow its Java where they differ) for every path in both teams' folders from their ideal and other starting states: the same states to 1e-13. Before, a point-mass profile (the settings' default constraints, a wheelCOF x g curve limit, no turning in the modules, no max angular velocity) ran 8793's paths 2.3-2.4 s quicker in all (Shuttle More 2.15 s, PathPlannerLib's 3.74 s) and 1114's 0.4-1.6 s quicker each, up to 3.8 m and 179° off it. `tools/team-autos.py` prints each path's length, time and which trajectory it ran.

**Starting orientation and rotations.** A team AUTO from PathPlanner, Choreo or Quikplan starts facing as their first path does (`startYaw`, blue field degrees: 0 faces the NEUTRAL ZONE): 8793's paths start at 180° (-178.5° on the left), facing backward, its intake toward its own ALLIANCE WALL; it starts on the line under the TRENCH's arm and drives out under it back first (its intake lowered as it sets off, as their TrenchMode does; up, the TRENCH would push it down: see 8793's section), turns its intake to lead down the CENTER LINE (-115° / 115°), comes home facing out (0°) and turns into the corner (180°) and along the wall (-110° / 110°). 1114's start at 45° (right) / -45° (left), 581's at -90° (left) / 90° (right), and 604's and 1678's at 0° (three of 604's at ±90°). Along the way the chassis follows their headings (`rot` on each point), not the direction it drives; a wait or held shot keeps the heading its trajectory left it at. Other AUTOs start facing the NEUTRAL ZONE and face the way they drive.

**Following by the clock.** These AUTOs run as their followers do (PathPlanner's FollowPathCommand with PPHolonomicDriveController, Choreo's AutoTrajectory, 604's FollowQuikplan): the setpoint is sampled at the time since the path started (where it should be, how fast, which way it faces), and the robot is commanded the setpoint's velocity plus P on how far it is from it and on its heading error, with their own gains (translation / rotation: 8793 5.0 / 5.0, 1114 3.5 / 5.0, 1678 6.0 / 4.0, 604 5.0 / 4.0, 581 3.5 / 7.5), capped at the robot's top speed and turn rate; its motors, grip and swerve modules limit how fast it gets there. Nothing is projected onto the path: a robot held up falls behind and is pulled after the setpoint as it moves on, one that's ahead is held back. Each path ends when its time is up, wherever the robot is, and the AUTO goes on to its next step (a robot blocked at the CENTER LINE doesn't sit there pushing). Consecutive paths are one trajectory, so the robot only stops where their paths end at 0 m/s or their autos stop for something (a shot, a wait): 8793's after Shuttle More, Shooting Corner, Depot Shoot and Center's first path; 1114's at each FullShoot (aiming from x = 3.1 m); 1678's at each fireCommand; 604's at its waits; 581's at each shot. `tools/team-auto-eval.mjs --track` prints the most the robot falls off its setpoint: within 0.06-0.23 m and 14-22° on 8793's and 1114's paths (1114's heading lags 18-22° where its paths turn 240°/s at 3 m/s), except where it's held up (8793's DEPOT, at 0.4-0.8 m and 40-75°, its intake down against the DEPOT and the wall); 1678's falls 0.75 m behind at 12.8 s, where its trajectory runs 4.38 m/s and the sim's 1678 tops out at 3.86, and lags 32° where driveOverBump's heading steps from their trajectory's; 604's within 0.3-0.55 m and 2-8°.

Their 581's points are replayed (`auto_581`): their follower's command, the robot's velocity turning toward it at up to 12 m/s², its speed and turn rate scaled down together where they'd ask the modules (0.41 m out) for more than 3.2 m/s, 90% of the sim's 581's top speed. At its very top speed it couldn't turn as well, fell behind, met the BUMP late and turned on its second crossing, and wedged its intake on the TRENCH's skirt for the rest of AUTO (20-27 AUTO FUEL, not 33-45). Their cluster-map lane choice, collision offsets and stuck-on-FUEL recovery hang on what the robot senses and are left out.

Every other AUTO (2910's drive-to-point, the BEST_AUTOS plans, the built-in routines, the Auto Editor's) drives its waypoints: it slows for a turn ahead, keeps off the walls by its own size (2910's TRENCH entry would pin the sim's against the wall), adds a path follower's position feedback when it falls behind, and a chassis-aimed shooter turns to its shot over the last 2.5 m. Each waypoint has a time budget, set as the robot heads for it: 1.5 times what a trapezoid at the step's speed and the drive's acceleration takes from where it is, plus 1 s; held up longer, it goes on to the next waypoint (and step) from wherever it is. Shots end when the robot's empty or at their time limit, waits at theirs, and an AUTO climb gives up if it can't get to its climbing spot in twice its drive time plus 2 s. The other robots run the highest-scoring AUTO found for them, with its own starting position (see BEST_AUTOS in `js/auto.js`); a robot with neither (none now; 581 before its own) runs the preload and a NEUTRAL ZONE sweep (it sat still, shooting its preload: 8-9 AUTO FUEL; 581 scores 43-57 that way). `tools/auto-search.mjs` finds these plans: it plays candidate plans headless against the other robots' best AUTOs on both alliances and keeps whichever scores the most AUTO FUEL. BUMPERS may reach past the CENTER LINE but never fully cross it, since G403 is only a foul for contact made fully across it. The AI runs this AUTO too.

## Auto Editor

**AUTO EDITOR** in the main menu is a simplified PathPlanner. The field (your half, ALLIANCE WALL on the left) is on the left; the settings are on the right; a bar along the bottom always shows the controls for what you're doing (for the controller or for the keyboard and mouse, whichever you're using).

- **Field:** drag the START box along the ROBOT STARTING LINE and place waypoints for the path. A label at the cursor says what A / Enter / a click does there (add waypoint 3, pick up waypoint 2, move START…). Arrows on the path show the direction; a legend under the field explains the colors (drive, intake, shoot, both, dashed = shoots or passes anywhere, thicker = faster). Waypoints past the CENTER LINE turn red as a G403 warning.
- **Settings, grouped:** *Auto* (which auto you're editing, the robot, New / Copy / Mirror / Rename / Delete), *Start* (starting spot, shoot the preload first), *Waypoint N* (on the way here: intake, shoot, speed; when it gets here: keep driving, stop, stop and shoot it all, wait 1–3 s), and *Path*, a list of every waypoint to jump to. The focused setting's description shows below them.
- **Time:** a bar at the top shows the estimated run time against the 20 s AUTO. **Test ▶** starts a match with the auto straight away; **Done** goes back.

Stick for the field, D-pad for the settings: whichever you touched last is active (outlined). Autos save automatically in the browser (localStorage) and appear in every **Auto routine** menu marked with ✎.

- With a custom auto selected, **Starting position** switches to *As drawn* / *Mirrored left ↔ right*.

## Real CAD

The field you see is FIRST's official field CAD (Onshape "FE-2026: REBUILT Playing Field"), in `cad/field/field.glb`. It's slimmed for the browser: the FUEL, carpet, tape and small hardware are dropped, and it has about 360k triangles in 26 meshes. The bleachers, HUMAN PLAYERS, HUB lights and CHUTE DOORS are still drawn by the game. Physics still uses the colliders built from the dimensions in `js/constants.js`, which line up with the CAD. To go back to the drawn field, set `"enabled": false` on the entry in `cad/manifest.json`.

To refresh the field from Onshape (this needs API keys):
```
tools/onshape-export.sh /tmp/field-raw.glb
npm i --no-save @gltf-transform/core @gltf-transform/extensions @gltf-transform/functions meshoptimizer
node tools/slim-glb.mjs /tmp/field-raw.glb cad/field/field.glb
```

The robots with public CAD (2910, 971, 1678) are built from it: `tools/extract-parts.mjs` takes a robot's Onshape GLB export and a recipe (`cad/robots/971.json`, `1678.json`) and writes each moving part (body, intake, sliding hopper, turret, lid) as its own light GLB in the robot's frame, with its origin on its pivot or axis. The game loads them in the browser and moves them; the drawn stand-ins show until they arrive, and headless runs never load them. To rebuild 971's (needs the API keys; 1678's is the same with its document):
```
DID=cabaa0c1c77517916df80783 WID=48cb057db38a03cc202ff44e tools/onshape-export.sh /tmp/r971.glb 96844befd4591dac162d657b
node tools/extract-parts.mjs /tmp/r971.glb cad/robots/971.json
```

Where a team's document doesn't allow export (1690's Onshape document, before their X_T release was imported), `tools/onshape-views.py` fetches Onshape's own shaded renders of it at a known scale and center, to measure a procedural model from (`cad/README.md`); nothing of it is committed.

Other CAD, such as robots or field elements, can be added the same way. Export glTF (.glb) from Onshape, or convert STEP with `python3 tools/cad2glb.py in.step out.glb`. Then list the file in `cad/manifest.json` (see `cad/README.md`).
